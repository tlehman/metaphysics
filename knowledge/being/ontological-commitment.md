[Quine](../lineage/quine.md)'s answer to *what is there?* is famously
unhelpful — "everything" — and his real contribution is the method for saying
more.

1. Take your best total theory of the world.
2. Regiment it into first-order logic.
3. A theory is committed to whatever must lie in the range of its bound
   variables for it to be true.

"To be is to be the value of a variable." Names and predicates commit you to
nothing; only quantification does. So paraphrase is the ontologist's tool: if
"there are prime numbers greater than 100" can be rewritten without quantifying
over numbers, the commitment evaporates. If it cannot, it stands.

## What it changed

The criterion made ontology a question about *theories* rather than about
intuitions, which is why it is the engine of the [indispensability
argument](../mathematics/indispensability-argument.md): our best science
quantifies ineliminably over mathematical objects, so we are committed to them.
[Field](../mathematics/mathematical-nominalism.md)'s nominalism attacks exactly
the ineliminability, taking step 3 for granted.

## What it flattens

Quine's *being* is univocal, all-or-nothing, and carries no structure —
precisely what Aristotle denied with [pros hen
equivocity](../aristotle/pros-hen-equivocity.md) and what the revival of
[grounding](grounding.md) and [fundamentality](fundamentality.md) reintroduces.
Carnap's earlier objection ran the other way: existence questions internal to a
framework are trivial, external ones are merely practical choices of framework,
so neither is the deep question Quine takes them to be.
