Aristotle's [essence](../aristotle/essence.md) answers *what* a thing is. It does
not, on its own, raise the question *whether* it is: the *Metaphysics* has no
separate notion of an act of existing. The distinction is
[Avicenna](../lineage/avicenna.md)'s, and it is the single most consequential
medieval addition to the Aristotelian apparatus.

## The argument

Consider the nature *horse*. Considered in itself it is neither one nor many,
neither existing nor non-existing; that a horse exists is a further fact not
contained in what a horse is. So in every creature, existence is distinct from
essence and is received.

From this Avicenna divides beings exhaustively:

- **Possible in itself** — whose essence does not entail existence, and which
  therefore requires a cause to exist. Everything we encounter.
- **Necessary in itself** — whose essence *is* its existence, requiring no
  cause.

The chain of possible beings, each existing through another, requires something
necessary in itself, and that is God — an argument from the structure of
existence rather than from motion. Contrast
[Aristotle's mover](../aristotle/unmoved-mover.md), which explains why things
*move* eternally, not why anything *is*.

## Aquinas's version

[Aquinas](../lineage/aquinas.md) sharpens it into a real composition of
*essentia* and *esse* in every creature, where *esse* — the act of being — is
the actuality of all acts, and creatures *participate* in a being that God
simply *is*. God's essence is His existence: *ipsum esse subsistens*. That
identity is the core of [divine simplicity](divine-simplicity.md) and the
Thomistic form of [pure actuality](pure-actuality.md).

## Dissent

Averroes rejected the distinction as a confusion of logic with metaphysics —
"existent" adds nothing to the thing. Scotus and Suárez reduce it to a
conceptual or modal distinction rather than a real composition. The
Frege–Russell view that [existence](../being/existence.md) is a second-order
property is a distant descendant of the deflationary side, and it dissolves the
argument entirely: if existence is not a property of individuals, no individual
can have it essentially.
