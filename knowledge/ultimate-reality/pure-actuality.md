The scholastic name for what
[Θ.8's stricter argument](../aristotle/priority-of-actuality.md) forces. Anything
with a potentiality to be is capable of not being, hence perishable; what is
eternal must therefore be fully actual. Λ.6 draws the conclusion for the first
principle: "there must be such a starting-point, the very substance of which is
activity," and such substances "must be without matter"
([unmoved mover](../aristotle/unmoved-mover.md)).

## What the scholastics derive from it

Aquinas treats *actus purus* as the premise from which the divine attributes
follow, one line each:

| Attribute | Because |
|---|---|
| Immutable | change is the actualisation of a potential; there is none |
| Eternal | time measures change; no change, no temporal succession |
| Immaterial | matter is [potentiality](../aristotle/potentiality-and-actuality.md) |
| [Simple](divine-simplicity.md) | composition is potentiality for the parts' being otherwise arranged |
| Necessary | what has no potentiality not to be, cannot fail to be |

Combined with the [essence/existence
distinction](essence-existence-distinction.md), it yields the Thomistic
identification: a being with no potentiality has no unrealised *esse*, so its
essence just is its existence.

## The strain

The doctrine is severe, and the classical objections all press on the same
place — a purely actual being cannot be affected, cannot respond, and cannot
change its mind, which is hard to reconcile with a God who acts in history or
answers prayer. Process theology and open theism reintroduce potentiality for
exactly this reason. Aristotle himself has no such problem, because his god does
not act on the world at all; it moves as an object of desire and is not aware of
anything but itself.
