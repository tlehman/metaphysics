"Why, then, is this one and not many — animal and two-footed?" (1037b13–14).
The problem is not linguistic: Aristotle concedes we might have coined a single
word (*himation*, "cloak") for *pale man*, and it still would not make the
formula a definition or *pale man* an essence (1029b28, 1030a2).

[The method of division](definition-by-division.md) reduces a chain of
differentiae to the last one, but leaves genus and differentia as two.

## Η.6's answer

Reserve it for wholes that are not heaps — "where the totality of them is not
like a heap, but the whole is something beyond the parts" (1045a8–10). The
puzzle dissolves, Aristotle says, once one sees "there is on the one hand matter
and on the other shape, and the one is potentially and the other actively"
(1045a20–25).

Suppose *round bronze* defines "cloak." Bronze is the matter, roundness the
form; the bronze is *potentially* round and *actually* round once it has the
form. There are not two things here. And since the compound was produced, there
is no cause of its unity beyond the agent who put the form into the matter.

## Extending this to universals

Definables are universals, and universals are not material objects — so how can
they be [hylomorphic compounds](hylomorphism.md)? Aristotle has the tool ready:
**intelligible matter** (*hulê noêtê*), introduced chiefly so that pure
geometrical objects not realised in bronze or stone have something quasi-material
to be made of. Elsewhere he says outright that "the genus is the matter of what
it is said to be the genus of" (Ι.8, 1058a23).

So a species is a compound after all: its matter is its genus, potentially some
specific kind or other; its differentia is the form that actualises it. The
genus *animal* is just what is potentially some specific animal, and does not
exist independently of its species any more than bronze exists apart from all
form. "The ultimate matter and the form are one and the same, the one
potentially, the other actively … and what potentially is and what actively
is are in a way one" (1045b17–21).

The solution covers only compounds, which Aristotle says is enough: "things that
have no matter … are all unconditionally just what is a one" (1045b23) — a
line that matters for the [unmoved mover](unmoved-mover.md), which has no
matter.
