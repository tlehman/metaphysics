Aristotle is a realist about scientific knowledge: the structure of a theory
must mirror the structure of reality, so scientific first principles must also
be reality's basic building blocks. Drive a wedge between the two and
scepticism follows — Descartes' dreaming argument is one such wedge.

B.6 sets the dilemma out (1003a7–17):

> We must … ask whether [the starting-points] are universal or exist in the
> way we say particulars do. For if they are universal, they will not be
> substances. For no common thing signifies a this something but a such-and-such
> sort of thing, whereas substance is a this something.… But if they are not
> universals, but exist as particulars, they will not be scientifically
> knowable. For scientific knowledge of all things is universal.

Both horns bite. Reality's building blocks are particular matter-form
compounds; science's starting-points are the universal
[essences](essence.md) of such things. There is no science of you or me, only
of human beings. See also [Ζ.13's attack on
universals](universals-and-substance.md), which is the same wound seen from
the ontological side.

## The route to a resolution

The investigation therefore starts with form, "the factor common to all
substances" (Ζ.3, announced 1029b3–12, completed only at the end of Θ.5, by
which point form is identified with [actuality](potentiality-and-actuality.md)).
But focusing on form leaves being fragmentary unless the forms of all substances
are explanatorily connected. Λ supplies the connection: matter-form compounds
depend on substantial activities, the celestial activities on a single
[prime mover](unmoved-mover.md), the sublunary world on the superlunary, and
[mathematical objects](mathematical-abstractionism.md) — in Books M and N — on
perceptible beings. Attributes depend on substances, compounds on forms, numbers
on compounds: vertical and horizontal unification complete.

## M.10's answer

> For scientific knowledge, like knowing scientifically, is twofold, one
> potential, the other active: the capacity, being as matter, universal and
> indefinite, is of what is universal and indefinite, whereas the activity,
> being definite, is of what is definite. (1087a10–25)

You and I may both know the same universal form, as we may both know the letter
A; but when I *actively* contemplate it, what is before my mind is a particular —
this actualisation of that universal. Now the primary god is eternally and
essentially the object of the active understanding that he is. So he is a
substantial particular; and being essentially an activity, he is also a
universal essence of a special sort, one that can only be actual and never
merely potential. He therefore stands on both sides of the divide, unifying
being and the knowledge of being at once, which is what realism required.
