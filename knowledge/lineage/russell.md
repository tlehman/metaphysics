Russell's place in this bundle is fixed by two months in 1901–2: the discovery
of [the paradox](../mathematics/russells-paradox.md) in the spring of 1901 —
he dated it variously to May and to June, in three different memoirs — and the
letter to [Frege](frege.md) of 16 June 1902 that arrived while the second volume
of the *Grundgesetze* was in press.

What matters philosophically is that he found it by *doing mathematics*, not by
looking for trouble: applying Cantor's diagonal method to "a supposed class of
all imaginable objects." He was convinced there is a universal class *V*, and
the paradox is what the diagonal argument leaves over when you assume there is.

## The rest of the response

- **The no-classes theory.** "Classes are entirely superfluous" (to Frege, May
  1903), made workable by the theory of denoting phrases in "On Denoting"
  (1905): definite descriptions have no meaning in isolation, so apparent
  reference to classes analyses away into quantification over propositional
  functions.
- **[Type theory](../mathematics/type-theory.md).** The vicious circle
  principle, simple types (1903) then ramified (1908), and *Principia
  Mathematica* with Whitehead (1910–13).
- **The failure that mattered.** Ramification needs the axiom of reducibility,
  which no one could call logical — so [logicism](../mathematics/logicism.md)
  arrives at set-theoreticism rather than at logic.

His view of [existence](../being/existence.md) as second-order, inherited from
Frege and sharpened by the theory of descriptions, is the other thing this
bundle owes him; it is what makes "there are no such things as classes" a
coherent thing to say rather than a self-refuting one.
