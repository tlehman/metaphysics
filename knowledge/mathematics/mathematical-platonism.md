Three conjuncts, each doing work:

1. **Existence.** There are mathematical objects — $\exists x\,\mathrm{Math}(x)$.
2. **Abstractness.** They are [abstract](abstract-objects.md) — outside space
   and time, and therefore causally inert.
3. **Independence.** They do not depend on us, our language, thought, or
   practices.

Written out, the conjunction is:

$$\exists x\,\mathrm{Math}(x) \;\wedge\; \forall x\,\bigl(\mathrm{Math}(x) \rightarrow \mathrm{Abstract}(x)\bigr) \;\wedge\; \forall x\,\bigl(\mathrm{Math}(x) \rightarrow \mathrm{Indep}(x)\bigr)$$

Drop (3) and you get some form of constructivism
([intuitionism](intuitionism.md)); drop (2) and you get a physicalist or
psychologistic view; drop (1) and you get
[nominalism](mathematical-nominalism.md) or
[fictionalism](fictionalism.md).

## The weakenings, in order

The conjuncts can be shed one at a time, and the intermediate positions are
occupied. Dropping only Independence gives **object realism** — there are
abstract mathematical objects, but they may be ontologically dependent or
derivative, grounded in our practices or in facts that do not themselves
involve them. It is strictly weaker than platonism and inherits none of the
Independence-driven access problem, which is much of its appeal.

$$\text{platonism} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{object realism} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Existence}$$

Orthogonal to that ladder is [truth-value realism](truth-value-realism.md) — a
metaphysical thesis that is not an ontological one — and weaker again is
[working realism](working-realism.md), which is a methodology and no thesis at
all. Confusing the four is the commonest way to make an argument for platonism
look better than it is.

## Two readings of Independence

The third conjunct is the one that is hard to state precisely, and it has a
weak and a strong reading:

- **Counterfactual independence.** Had there been no intelligent agents, or had
  their language, thought or practices been suitably different, there would
  still have been mathematical objects — $\Box\,(\neg\exists\,\text{agents} \rightarrow \exists x\,\mathrm{Math}(x))$.
- **Robust independence.** Mathematical objects are metaphysically on a par
  with ordinary physical objects.

The counterfactual reading is cheap: it is satisfied by objects whose existence
is necessary for any reason at all, including ones that are
[grounded](../being/grounding.md) in something else, so it fails to secure the
analogy with chairs and electrons that platonism advertises. The robust reading
secures the analogy and pays the full epistemological bill. Much of the
contemporary literature lives in the gap, holding that mathematical terms have
abstract semantic values without those values being robustly independent.

## The two arguments for it

- **[The Fregean argument](fregean-argument.md)** — from classical semantics
  plus the truth of ordinary theorems. Needs nothing but the language.
- **[The indispensability argument](indispensability-argument.md)** — from the
  ineliminable role of mathematics in confirmed science.

Neither delivers all three conjuncts. Both deliver Existence; Abstractness and
Independence have to be argued separately, which is a weakness rarely
advertised.

## The case for

- **Face value.** "There are infinitely many primes" is a true sentence of the
  standard form, and semantics should not be rewritten to avoid its obvious
  reading.
- **[Indispensability](indispensability-argument.md).** Our best science
  quantifies over mathematical objects and cannot be paraphrased free of them;
  by [Quine's criterion](../being/ontological-commitment.md), we are committed.
- **Objectivity.** Mathematical practice treats open questions as having
  determinate answers we have not yet found — which is easiest to explain if
  there is something the answers are about.

## The case against

The [epistemological objection](benacerrafs-dilemma.md) is the serious one: if
the objects are causally isolated, no causal story about how our beliefs track
them is available, and it is unclear what non-causal story could do the job.
Field sharpens it into the [reliability challenge](reliability-challenge.md) —
the platonist owes an explanation of why mathematicians' beliefs come out true,
and cannot even sketch one. The two best-developed platonist answers both work
by loosening Independence: [plenitudinous platonism](plenitudinous-platonism.md)
makes the realm so large that it cannot be missed, and
[potentialism](mathematical-potentialism.md) makes it something we extend rather
than survey.

Second: Benacerraf's *metaphysical* objection, the
[identification problem](structuralism.md). If 2 is a set, which
set? Zermelo's {{∅}} and von Neumann's {∅,{∅}} both work perfectly and
disagree, so no fact seems to select one — which is Benacerraf's other paper and
the birth of [structuralism](structuralism.md). The structuralist reply invites
the objection back one level up: numbers would then have *only* structural
properties, and Benacerraf's own conclusion was that there can be no objects
like that — every object must have some non-structural properties as well.

## Aristotle's objection, which is still live

Aristotle's whole treatment ([Μ–Ν](../aristotle/mathematical-abstractionism.md))
is aimed at exactly this position: mathematical objects are not
[separate](../glossary/choriston.md) substances, they are perceptible magnitudes
considered as separated. Compare
[the theory of Forms](../ultimate-reality/theory-of-forms.md), which is where the
name comes from — though modern platonism keeps the abstractness and drops the
Forms' causal and evaluative roles entirely.
