The plenitude principle, in one line: the mathematical realm contains every
object it possibly could.

$$\Diamond\,\exists x\,\Phi(x) \;\longrightarrow\; \exists x\,\Phi(x)$$

Equivalently, for theories rather than objects: if $T$ is consistent, then $T$
is true of some part of the mathematical realm.

$$\mathrm{Con}(T) \;\longrightarrow\; \exists \mathfrak{M}\,\bigl(\mathfrak{M} \models T\bigr)$$

Balaguer's name for it is *full-blooded platonism* (FBP). It is a strengthening
of [platonism](mathematical-platonism.md), not a retreat from it — it says there
are *more* abstract objects, not fewer.

## The point: it disarms the access problem

The [reliability challenge](reliability-challenge.md) asks how mathematicians'
beliefs manage to track a realm they cannot causally touch. On plenitude the
question loses its grip, because there is no *targeting* left to explain. A
mathematician who lays down a consistent set of axioms cannot fail to describe
something; the only way to be wrong is to be inconsistent, and consistency is
checkable from here. Reliability reduces to a fact about us — that we tend to
write down consistent theories — plus a fact about the realm that holds
necessarily. No mysterious channel is required.

The same move settles the independence results. There is no single universe of
sets that [CH](set-theory-and-foundations.md) is about; ZFC + CH and ZFC + ¬CH
both hold, of different regions. Compare
[the mathematical universe hypothesis](mathematical-universe-hypothesis.md),
which takes an equivalent plenitude and adds that the structures are
*physically* real.

## The costs

- **Consistency is not enough for determinacy.** If every consistent theory is
  true of something, then "the natural numbers" does not pick out a unique
  structure, and arithmetic's apparent determinateness has to be explained away
  or given up. The [identification problem](structuralism.md) returns enlarged.
- **What is a "possible" object?** The principle leans on a primitive modality
  no less obscure than the objects it was meant to make safe.
- **Ontological extravagance.** Objections from parsimony are weak against a
  realm that is causally inert and costs nothing to expand — but the view still
  has to say why plenitude stops at consistency.
- **It may prove too much.** A principle that guarantees you cannot be wrong
  looks less like an epistemology than like a change of subject, which is the
  standard complaint: FBP secures reliability by making mathematical truth
  cheap.
