**First theorem (1931).** For any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory *T*
that interprets a modest fragment of arithmetic, there is a sentence *G_T* in
*T*'s language such that neither *G_T* nor ¬*G_T* is provable in *T*. (Rosser
strengthened the hypothesis from ω-consistency to plain consistency.) By
arithmetising syntax and diagonalising, *G_T* in effect says "I am not provable
in *T*" — and, read in the standard model, it is true.

**Second theorem.** *T* cannot prove Con(*T*), its own consistency, unless it is
inconsistent.

## What follows

- **[Hilbert's programme](formalism.md) fails as stated.** The finitary core
  cannot certify the consistency of the ideal part, since that core is itself
  inside a system that cannot prove its own consistency.
- **Truth outruns provability-in-a-fixed-system.** Tarski's undefinability
  theorem makes this exact: arithmetical truth is not arithmetically definable.
  For [platonism](mathematical-platonism.md) this is congenial — mathematical
  truth is not made by our axioms — and for
  [formalism](formalism.md) and [fictionalism](fictionalism.md) it is a problem
  about which story is *the* story.
- **[Set theory](set-theory-and-foundations.md) is permanently open.** No
  effective axiomatisation will settle everything, so new axioms are always
  eligible.

## What does not follow

Not that "there are truths we can never know" — *G_T* is provable in stronger
systems, and we know it is true by reasoning about *T* from outside. Not, without
substantial further premises, that minds surpass machines: the Lucas–Penrose
argument requires that we know our own consistency and that our reasoning is
formalisable in a single system we can specify, both of which are contested.
