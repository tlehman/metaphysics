Fictionalism accepts the platonist's *semantics* and rejects the ontology. "2 is
prime" means what it appears to mean — it is about the number 2 — and it is
false, because there is no number 2. What is correct about it is that it is
*true in the story of mathematics*.

This is what distinguishes fictionalism from the reinterpretation strategies:
[structuralism](structuralism.md) and modal
[nominalism](mathematical-nominalism.md) change what the sentences say;
fictionalism leaves them alone and denies their truth.

## The pressure points

- **Application.** If it's false, why does it work? Field's answer is
  [conservativeness](mathematical-nominalism.md): a fiction that adds no new
  concrete conclusions is a safe inference-shortener. Critics answer that some
  mathematical explanations of physical facts look genuinely explanatory, not
  merely indexing (see [indispensability](indispensability-argument.md)).
- **Objectivity.** Fictions are made up; mathematics is not negotiable. The
  fictionalist owes an account of why the continuum hypothesis is not settled by
  stipulation the way Holmes's address is. The usual answer appeals to
  consequence: what is true in the story is what follows from the principles,
  which pushes the objectivity back into logic.
- **Which story?** [Gödel](godel-incompleteness.md) guarantees no consistent
  effective axiomatisation settles everything, so "the story of arithmetic" is
  underdetermined in exactly the places mathematicians most want answers.

Aristotle's treatment of mathematical objects "as separated"
(*hôs kekhôrismenon*) is a genuine fictionalism of the same shape, restricted to
separateness rather than to existence — see
[abstractionism](../aristotle/mathematical-abstractionism.md).
